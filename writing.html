What I Learned Building a Data Warehouse From Real-World Data

The project started as a checklist. It became a lesson in business processes, data quality, dimensional modeling, and the difference between making a calculation and representing the business behind it.

It didn't start as a data warehouse.

When I entered environmental consulting operations, I was learning an unfamiliar industry while trying to keep a complicated flow of projects organized. Information lived in job files, spreadsheets, laboratory reports, payroll records, invoices, contracts, and accounting records.

I started with a simple completion checklist inside each job file: do we have what we need to finish this job, produce the report, and request payment?

That small checklist solved an immediate problem. Then it exposed the next one.

The checklist became a system.

I built separate operational workbooks for project status, payroll, laboratory tracking, administrative approvals, technician licenses, supplies, and private-client work. Each began as a practical solution to a specific problem.

The systems grew because the business questions grew. Once labor was organized, I could ask about project cost. Once laboratory activity was organized, I could connect samples to projects and billing. Once project and billing records were connected, management questions became possible.

The company already had data. The hard part was connecting it.

Then the business exposed the limits of the model.

As I tested project-level profitability, I discovered that not every cost lived in the same place. Management labor was not represented in the ordinary payroll feed in the same way as field labor, and fixed or other project costs could sit outside the initial labor-and-lab view.

That changed the job. The goal was no longer “make the numbers work.” It was “make sure the model represents the business closely enough that people can trust the answers.”

That's where dimensional modeling came in.

I started defining grain, business keys, surrogate keys, dimensions, facts, historical rate logic, ETL dependencies, validation rules, and KPI definitions. I built fact tables for labor, laboratory activity, billing, and job-level summary information.

The architecture evolved as my understanding improved. Tables changed. Relationships changed. Business definitions changed. Testing the model was part of designing it.

The lesson I keep.

Technology was never the hardest part. The hardest part was understanding how information moved through the business and recognizing that every spreadsheet was really a representation of a business process.

Learning the vocabulary of analytics and data engineering didn't start the work—it gave me a way to describe and improve work I was already doing.

I kept solving real business problems until I accidentally wandered into analytics engineering.
